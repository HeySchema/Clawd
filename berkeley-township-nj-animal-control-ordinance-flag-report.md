## Detailed Line-by-Line Analysis

---

### FLAG 1: Incorrect Statutory Citation in ACO Duties (MODERATE)

**Ordinance Text (9-1, ACO Duties):**
> "The duties of the Animal Control Officer include enforcement of the measures and provisions for the control of dogs contained in **N.J.S.A. 4:19-15 through 4:10-15.29** and any amendments thereof, and the provisions of this section."

**Issue:**
The citation "4:10-15.29" is a **typographical error**. The correct citation is **N.J.S.A. 4:19-15.29**. The reference "4:10" does not correspond to any relevant title of New Jersey statutes governing animal control.

**Legal Impact:**
While likely a scrivener's error that a court would interpret charitably, this creates ambiguity about the precise scope of the ACO's enforcement authority. An officer relying on this citation could be directing enforcement to nonexistent statutory provisions.

**Recommendation:** Amend to read "N.J.S.A. 4:19-15 through 4:19-15.29."

---

### FLAG 2: General Penalty Exceeds State-Mandated Range (HIGH -- POTENTIALLY ILLEGAL)

**Ordinance Text (9-1, Penalties):**
> "Any person who violates any of the provisions of this section shall, upon conviction, be subject to a fine **not to exceed $500** or **imprisonment for a term not to exceed 90 days**, or both."

**Applicable State Law (N.J.S.A. 4:19-15.19 through 4:19-15.23):**
- First offense for licensing violations: **$1 to $50**
- Subsequent offenses: **$5 to $50**
- Confinement for non-payment: up to **10 days** (first), up to **30 days** (subsequent)

**Issue:**
Berkeley Township's general penalty of up to **$500** is **ten times the statutory maximum** of $50 for dog licensing violations under state law. The imprisonment term of up to **90 days** also far exceeds the state's 10-30 day confinement provisions.

To the extent this penalty is applied to conduct governed by N.J.S.A. 4:19-15 et seq. (e.g., failure to license, running at large, tag violations), **the local penalty exceeds the state-authorized range and is likely unenforceable** for those specific violations.

**Note:** Municipalities do have general penalty authority under N.J.S.A. 40:49-5 (up to $2,000 / 90 days for ordinance violations), but this general authority does not override specific penalty ceilings set by subject-matter statutes. Where state dog law specifies a penalty range, that range controls.

**Recommendation:** Restructure the penalty section to distinguish between:
- Violations governed by state dog law (subject to state penalty caps)
- Purely local regulatory violations (which may carry the higher municipal penalty)

### FLAG 3: Compounding Daily Fines for Licensing Failure (MODERATE -- DUE PROCESS CONCERN)

**Ordinance Text (9-1, Licensing):**
> "Failure of the owner to obtain a license within the time prescribed subjects the owner to a fine of $25 for the first offense. Any subsequent offenses are subject to fines at the discretion of the Municipal Court Judge. **Each day's failure to obtain a license is deemed a separate violation.**"

**Issue:**
The "each day is a separate violation" provision creates the potential for **exponentially compounding fines** for what is essentially a single continuing omission (failure to license a dog). A resident who is unaware of the requirement could theoretically accrue thousands of dollars in fines before receiving notice.

**Legal Concerns:**
- **Due process (14th Amendment / NJ Const. Art. I, Par. 1):** Compounding daily fines for a passive omission without adequate notice may be constitutionally disproportionate.
- **Excessive fines (8th Amendment / NJ Const. Art. I, Par. 12):** The cumulative effect of daily fines for a single ongoing omission could constitute an excessive fine.
- **State law comparison:** N.J.S.A. 4:19-15.19 imposes a single fine of $1-$50 for the first offense of failure to license, not a daily accruing penalty.

**Recommendation:** Remove "each day is a separate violation" language for licensing failures, or add a notice-and-cure provision requiring written notice before additional penalties accrue.

### FLAG 4: Potentially Dangerous Dog License Fee at Statutory Maximum (LOW -- LEGAL BUT NOTEWORTHY)

**Ordinance Text (9-1, Potentially Dangerous Dogs):**
> "The fee for the potentially dangerous dog license and each renewal thereof is **$700**."

**Applicable State Law (N.J.S.A. 4:19-24):**
> Municipalities may fix the fee "not less than $150 nor more than $700."

**Issue:**
While **technically legal**, setting the fee at the absolute statutory ceiling ($700) is noteworthy. The state default (absent local ordinance) is $150. Berkeley's fee is **4.7x the state default**.

**Potential Concern:**
Combined with other penalties in the ordinance, this could be argued as punitive rather than regulatory -- particularly for low-income residents who may have inherited or rescued a dog later deemed potentially dangerous. An equal protection or excessive fines challenge, while unlikely to succeed, is not frivolous.

**Recommendation:** No mandatory change required, but the Township should document the cost justification for the $700 fee.

### FLAG 5: Kennel License Fee May Overcharge Small Kennels (MODERATE -- POTENTIALLY ILLEGAL)

**Ordinance Text (9-3, Kennel Licensing):**
> Kennel license fee: **$25 per year** (flat fee for all kennels)

**Applicable State Law (N.J.S.A. 4:19-15.8):**
- Kennel with **10 or fewer dogs**: **$10**
- Kennel with **more than 10 dogs**: **$25**
- Pet shops: **$10**

**Issue:**
State law creates a **tiered fee structure** based on kennel size. Berkeley's flat $25 fee charges small kennels (10 or fewer dogs) **$15 more than the state-authorized amount** of $10.

For kennels with more than 10 dogs, the $25 fee is consistent with state law. But the ordinance does not distinguish between small and large kennels, meaning it **overcharges small operations** beyond the statutory limit.

**Recommendation:** Amend to implement the state's tiered fee structure: $10 for kennels with 10 or fewer dogs; $25 for kennels with more than 10 dogs.

### FLAG 6: Kennel License Expiration Date Conflicts with State Law (HIGH -- CONFLICTS WITH STATE LAW)

**Ordinance Text (9-3, Kennel Licensing):**
> "This license expires on the **first day of February** of each year and must be renewed prior to that time."

**Applicable State Law (N.J.S.A. 4:19-15.8):**
> Kennel and pet shop licenses "shall expire on the **last day of June** of each year."

**Issue:**
The local expiration date (February 1) **directly conflicts** with the state-mandated expiration date (June 30). This is not a matter of local discretion -- the state statute specifies the expiration date.

**Legal Impact:**
- A kennel operator who renews by February 1 (per local ordinance) but not by June 30 (per state law) could face conflicting compliance obligations.
- Conversely, an operator who relies on the state deadline could be penalized locally for lapsing in February.
- This conflict creates legal uncertainty and could be challenged as preempted by state law.

**Recommendation:** Amend the expiration date to June 30 to conform with N.J.S.A. 4:19-15.8.

### FLAG 7: Kennel License Process -- Board of Health Review and Neighbor Notification (LOW -- PERMISSIBLE)

**Ordinance Text (9-3, Kennel Licensing):**
> "Upon receipt, the Clerk forwards a copy to the Township Board of Health for its report and recommendation. The applicant must notify all bordering residents 10 days before approval of the license."

**Issue:**
State law does not require Board of Health review or neighbor notification for kennel licenses. However, this is likely a **permissible local addition** -- municipalities generally have authority to impose additional procedural requirements so long as they do not conflict with state law.

**Potential Concern:**
The neighbor notification requirement could be challenged as an **unreasonable barrier** to kennel licensing if used as a de facto veto power by neighbors. The ordinance should clarify that neighbor notification is for informational purposes and does not create a right of objection.

**Recommendation:** No mandatory change, but clarify that neighbor notification does not constitute a consent requirement.

### FLAG 8: Wildlife Feeding Prohibition on Private Property (MODERATE -- CONSTITUTIONAL CONCERN)

**Ordinance Text (Part III, 9-15 et seq.):**
> "No person shall feed, cause to be fed, or provide food for geese, ducks or other waterfowl on **publicly or privately owned lands** within the Township."

**Issue:**
While the prohibition on public lands is a straightforward exercise of municipal police power, extending the ban to **all private property** raises constitutional concerns:

- **Property rights (NJ Const. Art. I, Par. 1; U.S. Const. 5th/14th Amendments):** A blanket prohibition on feeding waterfowl on one's own private property interferes with a property owner's right to use their land. This is typically only permissible when the conduct constitutes a nuisance or poses a genuine public health/safety risk.
- **Overbreadth:** The ordinance does not distinguish between a property owner feeding ducks in their own backyard pond (minimal public impact) and someone feeding large flocks near public waterways (genuine nuisance potential).
- **Rational basis:** The ordinance states its purpose is to "protect the public's health, safety, and welfare," but the connection between private feeding of waterfowl on private land and public health/safety may not be sufficiently established.

**Note:** NJ courts have generally upheld wildlife feeding restrictions on public property. Extension to private property is less clearly supported.

**Recommendation:** Consider narrowing the private-property prohibition to situations that create a demonstrable public nuisance, or limit it to properties within a specified distance of public waterways or parks.

### FLAG 9: Inconsistent Penalty Amounts in Part III (MODERATE -- DRAFTING AMBIGUITY)

**Reported Ordinance Text (Part III):**
- One provision states: "Any person found in violation is subject to a fine **not to exceed $250** for each violation."
- Another provision states: "Any person found in violation is subject to a fine **not to exceed $500** for each and every violation."

**Issue:**
There appear to be **two different penalty maximums** within Part III (Other Animals). This creates ambiguity about which penalty applies to which violation. If the $250 cap applies to wildlife feeding and the $500 cap applies to pet waste violations (or vice versa), the ordinance should clearly delineate which penalty goes with which section.

**Recommendation:** Review and clarify Part III to ensure each prohibition has a single, clearly identified penalty.

### FLAG 10: State Preemption of Local Dangerous Dog Procedures (HIGH -- PREEMPTION RISK)

**Applicable State Law:**

**N.J.S.A. 4:19-36 (Preemption Provision):**
> "The provisions of this act shall supersede any law, ordinance, or regulation concerning vicious or potentially dangerous dogs, any specific breed of dog, or any other type of dog **inconsistent with this act** enacted by any municipality, county, or county or local board of health."

**State v. Smith (NJ Case Law):**
The court held that the Vicious and Potentially Dangerous Dog Act **preempts** any municipal ordinance dealing with the same subject. The state statute constitutes the **exclusive law** in this area. The court specifically found that:
- Municipal ordinances with independent procedural features for dangerous dog determinations **conflict with the state Act**
- Failure to follow state procedures **deprives the municipal court of subject matter jurisdiction**

**Issue with Berkeley's Ordinance:**
Berkeley's ordinance requires that owners of potentially dangerous dogs "obtain a potentially dangerous dog registration number and identification tag along with a license upon proof of compliance with Municipal Court orders." While this appears to reference and defer to the state Act, the following risks exist:

1. **If the ordinance creates any independent local procedures** for determining whether a dog is potentially dangerous (beyond what the state Act prescribes), those procedures are **preempted and void**.
2. **If the ordinance imposes additional requirements** on owners of potentially dangerous dogs beyond those in the state Act, those requirements may be preempted.
3. **If municipal court proceedings** under this ordinance deviate from the procedures in the state Act, the municipal court may lack **subject matter jurisdiction** (per State v. Smith).

**What the State Act Requires:**
- Impoundment of the dog
- Hearing before the municipal court
- Specific conditions upon owner (confinement, muzzling, liability insurance of $250,000-$500,000, warning sign, tattooing of registration number)
- The municipality cannot create its own parallel or alternative process

**Recommendation:** Conduct a detailed review of the full ordinance text to ensure no local procedures or requirements conflict with or supplement the state Act's dangerous dog framework. Any provisions that deviate from N.J.S.A. 4:19-17 through 4:19-36 should be repealed.

## Additional Observations

### Missing Provisions

The following provisions are **required by state law** but their inclusion in Berkeley's ordinance could not be confirmed from available text:

1. **7-day impoundment hold (N.J.S.A. 4:19-15.16):** State law requires shelters to hold impounded animals for at least 7 days before adoption or euthanasia. The ordinance should reference or incorporate this requirement.

2. **Microchip scanning (N.J.S.A. 4:19-15.16):** State law requires facilities to scan for microchips upon intake and before release. Not confirmed in ordinance.

3. **ACO certification requirement (N.J.S.A. 4:19-15.16a-b):** State law requires animal control officers to be certified through state-approved training. The ordinance defines an ACO as someone "who qualifies to perform such duties under the laws of the State" but does not explicitly reference the certification requirement.

4. **Pet shop license fees:** If the ordinance applies the $25 kennel fee to pet shops, this exceeds the state-mandated $10 fee for pet shops (N.J.S.A. 4:19-15.8).

### Breed-Specific Legislation

Berkeley's ordinance does not appear to contain breed-specific language, which is correct. Any breed-specific provisions would be **preempted** under N.J.S.A. 4:19-36 and the broader NJ prohibition on breed-specific legislation.
